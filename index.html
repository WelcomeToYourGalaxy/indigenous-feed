What this is. A feed on Indigenous, tribal and traditional peoples and the ground they hold: territory and titling, extraction and infrastructure on that territory, courts and treaties, defenders and the criminalisation of protest, peoples living in isolation, what is done to homelands and to health, eviction and displacement, language and heritage, and recognition and self-rule. Nothing here is written or summarised: headlines and snippets are the publishers' own, every row links to the original, and no model touches the text.

Both directions. Ground is taken — concessions granted, roads and dams built, communities evicted, rivers contaminated, leaders killed, protest criminalised. And ground is held or returned — land titled and demarcated, treaties settled, courts ruling for communities, co-management agreed, ancestors repatriated. Every row is marked with which it is, and the Direction filter separates them. A story can be both: a ruling won against a concession already granted.

Region. Placement runs three levels deep. Pick a region and a second row opens with its subregions; pick one of those and a third row lists the places inside it — Latin America to the Amazon Basin to the Brazilian Amazon, Oceania to Australia to the Northern Territory, Europe to Nordic and Arctic Europe to Sweden. Each row counts what it holds, so an empty subregion is visible rather than hidden. A story naming a place files under the subregion and region above it too, and each row is labelled with the most specific placement known for it.

Who reports it. Wires are labelled by what kind of source they are. Indigenous media & orgs covers Indigenous-run outlets and Indigenous-led organisations — APTN, ICT News, National Indigenous Times, Cultural Survival, IWGIA, Amazon Watch — and can be read on its own, which is the point of labelling it. Bodies & courts is UN mechanisms, rights bodies and court reporting. Then research and rights groups, field press, and general press across 25 languages.

What gets in. A story has to name a people and a matter of ground. Either alone is not enough: a language festival with no territorial dimension does not qualify, and a mining permit with no community attached belongs to a different feed. Environmental harm counts when it lands on a homeland — a spill in a river a community drinks from is this feed's business.

Evidence. Each story is scored on what it carries: a documented act — a ruling, a title granted, an eviction, a killing, a concession (2); institutional material — a UN mechanism, a court, Human Rights Watch, Global Witness, IWGIA, satellite monitoring, peer review (2); a measured extent in hectares, families or per cent (1); a pending decision (1); a named place (1); and a primary source (1). At three or more the row is marked well documented.

What is refused. The word native in its software, advertising, linguistic and botanical senses; tribal as a design or reality-television term; and the sports franchises that carry these words, which otherwise flood every query. The status line reports how many stories each harvest refused.

Limits worth knowing. The gate is mechanical: it reads words, not meaning. Peoples whose names the term list does not carry will be missed unless a story also uses a general word, and the list cannot hold every one of the world's several thousand Indigenous nations — add the ones you follow to PEOPLE in harvest_indigenous.py. Standing is assigned per wire, not per article. Google News caps a query at roughly 100 results. Coverage is uneven by language and the counts show it rather than hiding it.

Every wire read on the last harvest, with its standing: what it returned, and how many of its stories were refused or dropped as too local. A wire that could not be reached says so rather than disappearing.
